First, Okamoto-san says that this talk was brought about by a comment made by a foreigner, and after two or three nights of thinking about it, he's prepared an explanation for the misconception.

Okamoto-san begins by saying that the truth is that the Japanese studied hard the U.S. market, which was the world's largest market for video games. In fact, CAPCOM opened a new studio called CAPCOM USA precisely with the intention of selling to the U.S.

Moreover, together with other studios like SQUARESOFT and NAMCO, CAPCOM USA wanted to understand the market, so they hired Joseph Peter Morris, a rather chubby man that surprised even Okamoto-san because of his weight, to take on the job of marketing. At the time, CAPCOM USA gave him quite a favorable contract as incentive, paying him 25 cents (or 30 yen) for each Famicom cartridge sold, given that the studio's bosses themselves still didn't know very well yet how many cartridges they could really sell. However, Okamoto-san points out that while 25 cents appear to be a small amount, they were actually talking about selling 1 million copies, which they'd release throughout the years.

Okamoto-san also says that the bosses at CAPCOM USA used to remark to the Japanese that the Japanese style of illustration wouldn't be able sell in the U.S. market, so they'd change it themselves, just like in the case of "Rockman," whose name was changed to "Megaman." As a result, an illustration for Rockman became one for Megaman in which the hero has a pistol in his hand.

Nonetheless, they at CAPCOM in Japan didn't have any other way but to accept, because they also had a tight schedule. Furthermore, due to the "ATARI Shock," few people would want to carry out the task, thinking that the Famicom console wouldn't sell very much. Thus, Joseph had been the person who was available to do the job, with the condition that the Japanese would give him a good contract.

Okamoto-san explains that in the U.S., when a studio formed a contract, it had to explicitly state the job as well as the salary, and the other party wouldn't need to do everything else that wasn't written. Okamoto-san therefore admits that during that time he still didn't know well this system, pointing out that if an employer forced a worker to do something outside the contract, the latter could sue him in court.

In addition, Okamoto-san explains that if CAPCOM could earn 30 billion yen, even though CAPCOM USA had forecasted only 10 billion yen at the start, the latter could receive compensation in the form of bonuses. This is why Okamoto-san emphasizes that although CAPCOM USA strived to earn only 10 billion yen, they could get more extra money that Okamoto-san himself paid. Moreover, they traveled in first class, while the Japanese like Okamoto-san only traveled business class.

Finally, Okamoto-san concludes that while, in sum total, CAPCOM couldn't earn a lot of money during the Famicom era, it is the CAPCOM that it is today precisely because of the steps that they had taken. However, he is still unsatisfied with the illustrations such as the one for a Street Fighter II pinball arcade machine, because the Japanese strived very hard to achieve success, like in the case of the T.M.N.T. (Teenage Mutant Ninja Turtles) by Konami. 

This is all. Okamoto-san also advises us to write our comments about this topic if we have anything to say.

> 80% of public hospitals in Japan aren't making money, what are the "last citadels" in the policy necessary to solve this problem? (Part 2 of 2)

> Translator: [Michael B. Syson](https://www.linkedin.com/in/michaelsyson/)

> Publication Date: 2026-10-09

## Last updated: 2026-10-09T12:02

## --

Public hospitals are the "last citadels" in providing medical care in any given locality. Among the 8,000 or so hositals in Japan, public hospitals occupy about 10%. Many of them who provide medical care in remote areas also have difficulty making a profit. For instance, while there are 353 hospitals (as of April 2025) "operating in remote areas" including faraway islands and mountaneous regions to which medical doctors are dispatched, about 60% of them are public hospitals.

Moreover, as a general hospital, they also provide emergency, pediatric and prenatal care. These departments must maintain a set of number of staff as well as medical facilities that can respond anytime, regardless of the actual number of patients. There isn't enough money to cover the medical staff's compensation alone. Since public hospitals also handle unprofitable cases, it's turned into a system that makes it difficult for them to make money.

From the current state of things, financial support from the national government would be welcome. Meanwhile, for hospitals that provide the necessary medical care in remote areas with a low population size, it would be wise to give supplementary financial aid and set sufficient taxation mechanisms in the area. However, using compensation for the medical treatment provided to measure the medical situation of the area isn't sound. From the standpoint of the residents, there's a higher possibility of even more medical care being needed in the remote areas than in the city. 

## --

"Losing money" isn't a given

## --

On the one hand, saying that it's a given that public hospitals are losing money is wrong. Instead, there must be a reform in the management of these hospitals. Having said this, what's important is that the staff are aware. For instance, linkages with the medical practicioners in the area should be strengthened, response toward emergency situations should be widened, and the number of patients seen should be increased. Also, hospitals that mainly take in emergency patients must raise their in-patient turn-over rates by transferring chronic or recovering patients to other hospitals or returned to their residences for home care in order to be able to provide a high level of medical care. This is the obvious strategy that hospital directors must take. On the side of the staff, they must help improve the workplace through a bottom-up approach. 

In certain regions, many hospitals in nearby areas have taken on the stance of "enduring longer," though this doesn't necessarily mean that they are competing among themselves. The Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare has provided a basic framework for a new regional way of medical care toward the year 2040 to get all areas in Japan to think about their role in hospital-hospital medical care. A system to provide medical treatment aligned with that idea must be made while looking at the future population size of those areas. It would also be necessary to discuss among the colleagues of neighboring prefectures what to do when patients must move between regions. A system that provides unused medical treatment only increases financial losses.

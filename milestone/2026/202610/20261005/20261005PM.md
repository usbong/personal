First, Okamoto-san admits that he doesn't know the original reason why the bucket list had been created, but he really liked the idea after hearing it.

He also explains that each person has a list that maybe doesn't include the goal that another person has in his own list. For example, in the list of 100 things that Ms. Tapi wants to do, there's the goal of seeing the aurora borealis, which isn't actually in Okamoto-san's list. Okamoto-san therefore highlights that there are objectives or goals that a person wants to do during a certain point in time, such as participating in a marathon for the reason that he's still young.

Okamoto-san gives another example, which is to experience playing soccer, or futsal in this case, with his son. Okamoto-san reveals that it was very challenging for him, because children moved very quickly, and he was already in his forties. He also recalls that his son wanted to throw a ball in baseball really well. Okamoto-san explains that the speed of a thrown ball has a maximum that is related to the person's age. Thus, a person who wants to reach the summit of Mt. Everest goes to train himself well in order to accomplish this task, while an old person in his sixties, perhaps, isn't anymore too interested in doing it.

Finally, Okamoto-san advises us to accomplish our goals, because to have a list like the bucket list is a way of encouraging us to strive to achieve them.

Before closing, Okamoto-san also wants to read our comments about our own lists, so let's write a comment about this topic too.

Therefore, I'd like to respond that I have in my bucket list the goal of proving that the financial machinery that I'm creating will be able to let me survive with the longest and best life possible unlike other people who spread the idea of rewarding those who don't strive including not coming on time.

> Stories from Game Producer Yoshiki Okamoto: When CAPCOM was struggling to make money by selling Famicom cartridges

First, Okamoto-san reveals that CAPCOM has experienced a lot of instances when it was almost going to go bankrupt, but was able to survive thanks to God.

Okamoto-san gives the example when CAPCOM was releasing games for the Famicom console as well the Super Famicom. He explains that while other shady factories existed to produce cartridges from the ROM that a studio like CAPCOM had created, they, developers, went to Nintendo whose official factory would help them produce the cartridges.

Okamoto-san recounts that they had to bring the ROM to the factory three months before releasing the game, because it needed so much time to accomplish the task. Furthermore, each cartridge cost them 1,850 yen, and because they asked the factory to produce 300,000 cartridges, they had to borrow money from the bank. However, given that, at times, they couldn't sell all the cartridges, the rest would be brought to the warehouse. Therefore, a difference in the sales forecast by 50,000 cartridges would result to their having to spend money to put them in the warehouse. Meanwhile, the bank continued to take the interest from the money they borrowed.

This is why Mr. Tsujimoto Kenzo, the CAPCOM President during that time, told Okamoto-san that they weren't making money selling games in cartridges. Okamoto-san agreed, noting that the task, he thought, was more for establishing the studio's brand.

Okamoto-san also recalls that each time they, the developers, encountered bugs, particularly the fatal ones, they had to wait 30 days again for the factory to be able to create the cartridges anew. Okamoto-san admits that he had to explain the situation to the factory manager that the bug would cause the screen to stop moving, meaning that it was really an error so fatal that they needed to fix it.

Okamoto-san next points out that CAPCOM USA, the CAPCOM studio in the U.S., had a situation quite different from Japan's, because the buyers of their cartridges in the department stores including Toyr "R" Us could return them within two weeks, such that they could try out a game of theirs like Megaman, return it to be exchanged for another game like Final Fantasy. Furthermore, the American bosses were able to get a lot of bonus money given the culture in the U.S. as well as travel in first class, while the Japanese couldn't enjoy these things at all, so much so that Okamoto-san wondered about the capability of CAPCOM USA to make money.

Finally, Okamoto-san concludes that CAPCOM has survived, thanks to God, many instances when it was almost going to go bankrupt. He counts four instances, and since the young people at CAPCOM currently count three instances when they had happeneed, they including Okamoto-san hope for the fourth time in which they could survive again thanks to God.

This is all. As always, Okamoto-san says that he wants to read our comments if we have any.
